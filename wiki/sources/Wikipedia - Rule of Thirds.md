# Wikipedia — Rule of Thirds

## Summary

Reference article on the [[Rule of Thirds]]. Defines the rule (divide the frame into nine equal cells; place compositional elements along the lines or at the four intersection points), describes its application in photography and film, and gives the actual 1797 origin in John Thomas Smith's *Remarks on Rural Scenery*. Cites George Field's 1845 *Chromatics* for an early skeptical critique of universalizing the rule.

## What it contributes

- The canonical definition with the standard 3×3-grid illustration.
- The **primary-source quotation** from John Thomas Smith (1797) showing the rule was originally about *proportion* — sky vs. land, light vs. dark in ratios of 2:1 — not a geometric grid for subject placement.
- The Sir Joshua Reynolds (1783) reference Smith was quoting (warm-to-cold colour proportions in painting).
- George Field's 1845 critique that the rule "does not supply a general law, but universalises a particular," foreshadowing modern skeptical readings.
- Cross-references to related composition concepts: Golden Triangle, Diagonal Method, Headroom, Lead Room.

## Key claims

- **high** The expression "rule of thirds" was first written by John Thomas Smith in 1797 in *Remarks on Rural Scenery*.
- **high** Smith was quoting Sir Joshua Reynolds (1783), whose original observation was about *colour proportion* in painting (warm to cold ≈ 2:1).
- **high** The current popular form of the rule — place subjects at the four 3×3-grid intersections — is the geometric reinterpretation, not Smith's original statement.
- **high** George Field's 1845 *Chromatics* contains an explicit skeptical critique: the rule "is neither accurate nor universal."
- **medium** The rule is "favored by cinematographers in their effort to design balanced and unified images" (citing Ryan & Lenos).
- **medium** Common application: align body to a vertical, eyes to a horizontal; for moving subjects, leave space in the direction of motion.
