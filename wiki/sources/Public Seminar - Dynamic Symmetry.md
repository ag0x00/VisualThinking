# Public Seminar — Dynamic Symmetry: A Mathematical Structure in New School History

## Summary

A historical essay by a mathematician on **Jay Hambidge's Dynamic Symmetry** — its mathematical content, its 1920s popularity at the New York School of Fine and Applied Art (later Parsons) and the New School, its application by muralist José Clemente Orozco, its critical reception, and its decline. Combines clean construction-level math with primary-source historical material from New School archives.

## What it contributes

- The **reciprocal-rectangle construction** explained step by step with figures: starting from a rectangle and its diagonal, derive the inscribed similar rectangle that gives the system its iterative ("dynamic") character.
- The relationship between root rectangles ($\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3}, \sqrt{5}$), the golden rectangle, and the [[Golden Spiral]] (the "whirling rectangles" sequence).
- The **critical history**: pushback from the *American Journal of Archaeology* (1921), *Art Bulletin* (1921), and mathematician Albert Bennett challenging Hambidge's precision and his lack of historical evidence for conscious classical use.
- Curriculum history: Dynamic Symmetry taught at Parsons in the 1920s–30s, at the New School in the 1920s under Ralph Pearson, and even in Brooklyn high schools. Disappeared by the 1940s.
- Orozco's use of Dynamic Symmetry in his New School murals — a documented intentional application.
- The thread to Le Corbusier's *Modulor* via Ozenfant.

## Key claims

- **high** Hambidge's *Dynamic Symmetry: The Greek Vase* was published Yale University Press, 1920.
- **high** The reciprocal-rectangle construction is geometrically exact: $BE \perp AC$ produces a similar inscribed rectangle.
- **high** Iterative application on a golden rectangle yields the whirling-rectangles spiral, an approximation of the [[Golden Spiral]].
- **high** Strong contemporary critiques were published in *AJA* and *Art Bulletin* (1921) challenging Hambidge's measurement precision and historical claims.
- **high** Dynamic Symmetry was integrated into Parsons / New York School of Fine and Applied Art curriculum from 1920 through the 1930s–40s.
- **high** Ralph Pearson recanted his enthusiasm for Dynamic Symmetry in the second edition of *How to See Modern Pictures*.
- **medium** Orozco's New School murals (1930–31) are documented examples of intentional Dynamic-Symmetry composition; biographer Lawrence Hurlburt did a detailed analysis of the left half of *Struggle in the Occident*.
