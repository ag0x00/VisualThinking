# Appraisal Theories of Emotion

**Appraisal theories** (Magda Arnold 1960; Richard Lazarus 1966+; Klaus Scherer 1984+) hold that emotions arise from **cognitive evaluations** of a situation along specific dimensions. The same event can produce different emotions in different people *because their appraisals differ* — not because the event has emotion baked in.

Where [[Russell's Affect Circumplex|dimensional]] and [[Plutchik's Wheel of Emotions|categorical]] models describe **what emotions are**, appraisal theories describe **how they are generated**. Together with [[Constructed Emotion Theory|constructionism]], appraisal theory is the contemporary mainstream account of emotion-causation.

For generative art and brand design, appraisal theory matters because it specifies **which features of a stimulus** trigger which emotions — at the level of *meaning*, not raw perception.

## The core appraisal dimensions

Different theorists name different dimensions, with substantial overlap. **Scherer's Component Process Model** (the most-developed) uses these *Stimulus Evaluation Checks*:

| Check | Question the system asks | Influences |
|---|---|---|
| **Novelty** | Is this expected? | All emotions; specifically surprise / interest |
| **Pleasantness** | Is this intrinsically pleasant or unpleasant? | Valence |
| **Goal-relevance** | Does this matter for my goals? | Arousal; emotional intensity |
| **Goal-congruence** | Does this help or hinder my goals? | Valence sign once goal-relevance is positive |
| **Agency** | Who caused this — me, another, or chance? | Anger (other), guilt (self), sadness (chance) |
| **Coping potential** | Can I do something about it? | Fear (no) vs anger (yes); challenge vs threat |
| **Norm/value compatibility** | Does this fit my norms or violate them? | Guilt, shame, pride, disgust |

A given event triggers a *combination* of these checks, and the combination determines the emotion.

## Lazarus's primary and secondary appraisal

The older, simpler framework (Lazarus 1966, 1991):

- **Primary appraisal**: Is this situation **relevant** to my goals? Is it **congruent** or **incongruent** with them? What's at stake?
- **Secondary appraisal**: What **resources** do I have to cope? Who is **responsible**?

A negative situation that is congruent-with-goals + I have resources = **challenge** (positive emotion despite difficulty).
A negative situation that is incongruent + I lack resources = **threat** (anxiety, fear).

This is why two people facing the same hard task feel completely different emotions — their primary and secondary appraisals diverge.

## Appraisal patterns for specific emotions

Scherer-style predictions for prototypical emotions:

| Emotion | Novelty | Pleasantness | Goal-cong. | Agency | Coping |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Joy** | low–mid | high | high | self | high |
| **Anger** | low | low | low (blocked) | other | high (act) |
| **Fear** | high | low | low | other/chance | low |
| **Sadness** | low | low | low (lost) | chance/self | low |
| **Disgust** | low | very low | low (violated) | other | mid |
| **Surprise** | very high | neutral | unknown | unknown | unknown |
| **Pride** | low | high | high | self | high |
| **Shame** | low | low | low (violated norm) | self | low |
| **Guilt** | low | low | low (harmed other) | self | mid |
| **Hope** | low | high | high (anticipated) | self/other | mid |

Each row is a *pattern* of appraisal that, on Scherer's account, produces the named emotion. **The pattern is the cause; the emotion is the consequence.**

## What appraisal theories add to the dimensional and categorical pictures

| Approach | Tells us | Doesn't tell us |
|---|---|---|
| Russell circumplex | Where the emotion sits in (P, A) space | Why this emotion was produced |
| Plutchik wheel | Which categorical label fits | What in the situation triggered it |
| **Appraisal** | **What features of the situation generated the emotion** | What the emotion *feels* like to experience |
| Constructionism | How concepts shape experience of core affect | The structural features of the eliciting situation |

For generative art, appraisal theory tells us **what to put in a stimulus to make a specific emotion likely**:

- To trigger **awe**: build novelty (unexpected scale, unfamiliar geometry) + pleasantness (resolved structure) + low coping potential (you can't change it; you can only behold it). Vast landscapes, cathedral interiors, the night sky.
- To trigger **anger**: build clear agency-attribution (an antagonist visible in the frame), goal-blockage (a desire frustrated), high coping potential (you could act). Editorial cartoons that depict an obstacle and its owner.
- To trigger **fear** (vs anger): same blockage but with **low** coping potential. Vermin in a corner; an approaching shadow with no escape.
- To trigger **hope**: anticipated positive event, with some agency, some uncertainty. Spring after winter; sunrise compositions.

## The structural-vs-cognitive question

Appraisal theory positions emotion-causation at the **cognitive** level: the *meaning* of the situation, not its raw perceptual features. This raises a question for visual art:

- Does an Arnheim-style rising-line + open-form composition produce **joy** because of the structural-pattern isomorphism ([[Expression as Configuration of Forces]])?
- Or does it produce joy because the viewer *appraises* the upward direction as "progress / aspiration / goal-fulfillment" (an appraisal-theory account)?

**Probably both, at different levels**:
- The **structural pattern** produces a core-affect response (Arnheim + Barrett substrate): positive valence + moderate arousal.
- The **appraisal** of the pattern in context (this is a poster about a graduation; this is the company's annual report) supplies the specific emotional category (joy / pride / hope).

This nests cleanly with [[Constructed Emotion Theory]]: structural pattern → core affect → appraisal → categorical label.

## Why this matters for the wiki's four priorities

| Priority | Use |
|---|---|
| 1. Generative art | When the brief specifies a particular *named* emotion (pride, hope, awe), appraisal theory tells us which structural and narrative features to put in the image. |
| 2. Branding | Brand emotional positioning is largely appraisal-engineering: "we make you feel competent" = build self-agency + coping-potential signals; "we make you feel safe" = build low-novelty + high-coping-potential. |
| 3. Graphic design | Editorial / advocacy work targets specific appraisals: anger at injustice, hope for change. Appraisal dimensions specify what to depict. |
| 4. Music-reactive visualizers | Less direct, since music supplies the emotion. But the visualizer can either align with or counter the appraisal of the music (an ironic juxtaposition is an *agency-shift* appraisal move). |

## Caveats

- Appraisal theories assume **conscious or semi-conscious cognitive evaluation**. This works for adult humans in non-emergency situations; it works less well for very fast automatic emotional reactions (the snake-startle, the disgust-recoil) which are pre-cognitive.
- The number and identity of "appraisal dimensions" varies across theorists (Scherer ~5–7; Smith & Ellsworth 6; Roseman 7; Lazarus 6). The **family** is right; the **specific list** is not yet consensus.
- Cross-cultural validation is moderate — the appraisal dimensions appear cross-culturally, but the *specific values* (what counts as a goal-blockage; who is the appropriate agent to blame) vary heavily.
