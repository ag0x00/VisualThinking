# Figure and Ground

The most fundamental act of seeing space is splitting a flat retinal image into **figure** (a bounded shape lying *on top of* something) and **ground** (a continuous surface lying *behind*). Rubin's 1921 study established the conditions; Arnheim's *Art and Visual Perception* (Ch V) generalizes those conditions to multi-level depth and connects them to the principle of perceptual simplicity.

## Rubin's rules (the factors that turn a region into figure)

In order of empirical strength:

1. **Enclosedness** — the enclosed region tends to become figure, the enclosing one becomes ground.
2. **Smaller area** — between two regions sharing a contour, the smaller area more readily becomes figure.
3. **Convexity over concavity** — a convex region wins over a concave one (e.g., Rubin's vase illusion: convex columns are seen as figure more often than the concave goblet between them).
4. **Symmetry / axial alignment** — figures whose main axes coincide with the vertical/horizontal of the visual field win over tilted ones.
5. **Texture / density** — more densely textured surfaces tend to lie closer to the observer (become figure). Empty / homogeneous patches recede.
6. **Brightness and hue** — short-wavelength colors (blue) tend to recede; long-wavelength (red) advance. Brighter surfaces more often supply the ground (evidence limited).
7. **Top/bottom orientation** — the lower portion of a frontal pattern tends to come forward (anisotropic visual field; lower half perceptually "heavier"; see [[Visual Balance]]).
8. **Contour ownership** — at a shared border, the contour belongs to the figure. The ground extends *unbroken* behind it.

## Arnheim's generalization: depth levels and the principle of economy

Two regions sharing a contour rarely fix into a stable two-level pattern. The perceptual system distributes regions across **as few depth levels as the configuration allows** (principle of economy). For example:

- A circle inside a square is preferentially seen as a square *with a circular hole* (two levels) rather than a disk on top of a square (three levels) — until other factors (texture, contrast) tip it.
- A pattern can split into 4+ depth planes (e.g., Hans Arp's woodcuts) when conflicting figure/ground cues balance.

The aim is always the **simplest total configuration** (fewest planes, simplest shape on each), constrained by the projective stimulus.

## Contour-sharing instabilities

When two regions share a border, the contour assumes a different *shape* depending on which side claims it (e.g., Picasso's *La Vie* — the same line is convex breast on one side, concave shoulder on the other). This is exploited in:

- **Cubist / surrealist** ambiguity (Braque, Picasso).
- **Trompe-l'œil** reversals (Dalí, Tchelitchew).
- **Decorative** interplay (Greek vase paintings — interstices between figures shaped to almost claim figure character).

## Programmable implications

- **Compositions** that want a stable, readable foreground must give it: enclosure, smaller area, convex outline, axial alignment, texture, and warm/bright color.
- **Hierarchy of attention** can be controlled by stacking these cues: a region that satisfies most cues will reliably read as the figure.
- **Negative space design** (see [[Compositional Grids]], [[Rule of Thirds]]) works by deliberately *failing* one or more cues on the dominant element, forcing the eye to do additional work and giving the interstice quasi-figure status.
- For a generative system, scoring a candidate composition can include a "figure-ground clarity" metric: how many of the 8 cues agree on which region is foreground.
